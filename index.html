Search Intelligence Capstone: From Search Data to Ranked Content Recommendations

Author: Shreya Srivastava

Evidence note: Every figure below comes from a fresh, reproduced run of scripts/run_all.py against the bundled anonymized dataset — no value is invented or estimated.

Abstract

Search behavior contains measurable signals about information demand and where content may be failing to satisfy it. This study builds a repeatable search-intelligence workflow on the FlyRank ML Internship content-performance dataset: data contract → features → baseline → model → leakage checks → validation → ranked recommendations.

Research question: Can 90-day search/engagement and content-context features separate declining-visibility content from stable content well enough to rank pages for editorial refresh — and beat a transparent hand-written rule?

Dataset: 30,000 content items · 32 pseudonymized clients · Period: trailing 90-day window ending at export · Final approach: random forest classifier, 52 features

Main result: The random forest reaches ROC AUC 0.750 and Precision@50 = 0.740, versus the rule-based baseline's ROC AUC 0.627 and Precision@50 = 0.240 — roughly a 3x improvement in top-of-queue precision, evaluated on a client-holdout split so no client's pages appear in both train and test.

1. Introduction

The objective is not merely to fit a model. The objective is to establish a defensible evidence chain from observable search behavior to an actionable content decision: what signal is present, whether it survives validation, and what a content team should do differently because of it.

2. Data

The study uses the anonymized starter export bundled with the FlyRank ML Internship repository — a small slice of the ~79M-row full release, not the full release itself.

  • Release: data/raw/content_refresh_anonymized.csv — anonymized starter slice
  • Observation window: trailing 90 days, ending at export time
  • Records: 30,000 rows, 32 pseudonymized clients (no rows dropped by filtering)
  • Unit of analysis: one row = one pseudonymized content item (page)
  • Target: is_declining_label = (trend_direction == "down"), positive rate 54.2%
  • Exclusions: zero-impression or <90-day-old items; provider_used/model_used; all client-identifying fields (already stripped upstream)

3. Methodology

The analysis follows a fixed sequence: research question → data contract → features → baseline → model/scoring → validation → interpretation → recommendations.

Features: 52 total (18 numeric — impressions/clicks/sessions logs, avg position, CTR, engagement/scroll rate, age, word/char count — + 8 one-hot categorical fields).

Baseline: transparent weighted rule — 40% visibility + 30% freshness risk + 25% position opportunity + 5% depth gap, with human-readable reason codes.

Final approach: random forest (vs. logistic regression and decision tree), selected by Precision@50 on a client-holdout split (20% of clients held out; 2,325 test rows / 27,675 train rows).

Leakage checks: the trend-derived columns that define the label (trend_direction, trend_pct, and the last-30d/prev-30d windows) are excluded from the feature set; splits are by client, not row, so no client appears in both train and test.

5. Limitations & Honest Framing

Search observations are observational signals. They do not, by themselves, prove why a page is declining or that refreshing it would cause recovery. Findings apply only to this 30,000-row, 32-client anonymized slice and a single 90-day snapshot — not the full ~79M-row release, other clients, or other periods.

Study-specific limitations: no forward-looking outcome window exists to test whether acting on the queue actually improves visibility; keyword-context missingness is systematically tied to content type and absorbed via an "unknown" tier rather than imputed.

7. Reproducibility

The primary reproducibility artifact is scripts/run_all.py, which runs the full five-stage pipeline against the bundled CSV — no external data access required. A fresh run during preparation of this paper reproduced every table and figure above exactly.

8. Conclusion

A random forest trained on 52 search-behavior and content-context features identifies declining-visibility content roughly 3x more precisely at the top of the queue than a transparent hand-written rule, under a client-holdout split designed to resist the most obvious leakage path. The value is the full evidence chain — explicit label definition, excluded leakage columns, a matched baseline, and an auditable reason-coded queue — not the model score alone. The study does not show that acting on the queue improves outcomes, since no post-action data exists yet to test that.
